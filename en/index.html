Carrying More Than Water

Rethinking the Success of Rural Water Investments Through Gender-Responsive Evaluation – A Case Study in Meo Vac, Viet Nam

By Nguyen Tra My · Tran Chuc Linh · Trinh Ho Ha An – Foreign Trade University

On a plateau where rain drains straight into the rock, a reservoir brought water to the centre of a village. It was counted as a success. This is the story of what it changed, and of what the counting left out.

Chapter 3

Water That Does Not Stay

Wet season

Wet season: rain falls

Rain is not what the plateau lacks. In the wet season it falls heavily, and households collect it and store it at home until it runs out.

Wet season

But it does not stay

The water drains through cracks and sinkholes in the limestone…

Wet season

It disappears underground

…and flows away underground, leaving few streams or ponds on the surface. Rivers such as the Nho Que run at the bottom of deep canyons, far below the villages.

Dry season

Dry season: carry it

When the stored rain runs out, the nearest source may be a thin flow from a crevice high in the rock, or water near the bottom of a ravine. People go down and carry it back in plastic cans on their backs.

Dry season

Dry season: buy it

Shops collect rainwater in the wet months, then buy water by the truckload, around 10 m³ at a time. “As the dry season approaches and the rainy season ends, water has to be bought continuously.” (Chu Minh Quang)

Chapter 4

From Stopgaps to a Reservoir

How households coped

For generations, households found their own way through each dry season. These are not stages that replaced one another: several are still in use today.

  1. 1 Still in use in 2026

    Searching the rock

    After a full day in the fields, people set out in the evening, through the forest and up the mountain, searching crevice by crevice for enough water to carry home for the next day. (A Seo Sa Lung villager’s account to a reporter.) Where water sources are distant, people still go down near the ravines to find water and carry it back in plastic cans on their backs.

  2. 2 Still in use in 2026

    Storing the rain

    Rainwater is collected and stored at home through the wet season, until it runs out. Donor-funded programmes, including one supported by the International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD), built rainwater tanks and gravity-fed water systems in parts of Ha Giang. Coverage remained partial.

  3. 3 Background (web source)

    “Hanging lakes”

    121 “hanging lakes”, reservoirs built high on the rock, now hold about half a million m³ of water on the plateau and serve some 100,000 people.

  4. 4 Still in use in 2026

    Pipes bought at the market

    Households bought long pipes at the market and ran them from sources higher up the mountain down to their homes. Getting the water flowing still took about an hour a day, but it no longer cut into the time needed for school or work.

  5. 5 Still in use in 2026

    Water by the truckload

    Shops collect rainwater in the wet months, then buy water by the truckload, around 10 m³ at a time, because there is no piped supply. Drilled wells cannot be used: the water is polluted and salty. Those with a vehicle could buy water; those without carried it.

  6. 6 Public investment

    A reservoir for the village

    Under Programme 1719, concentrated water works (reservoirs, tanks and pipe networks shared by whole villages) were funded for the karst villages of Meo Vac.

“Girls are more diligent”

10 of about 15

people interviewed described women and girls as taking on a wider range of work than men and boys: fetching water, washing clothes, working in the fields and selling at the market.

No one described water as a job reserved for women. It simply fell to them more often. No one called it a rule. To Nguyet, it sounded like an expectation, and in practice the expectation decided who carried the water.

Carrying water, then housework

For the women and girls Nguyet heard about, water was one task in a chain. Select each link, then find the one nobody counted.

Fetching and carrying it home, then rationing it between cooking, drinking and washing, often using it more than once before it was thrown away.

Once the water had been fetched, there were still the goats to look after, for at least an hour every day.

Work in the fields, on which most families depended for their income.

Washing clothes, one of the tasks interviewees described women and girls taking on.

For the youngest, school, with the trips for water fitted around lessons.

Someone had to notice when the store was running low, decide what the remaining water would be used for first, and decide when it was time to go for more. None of this was counted anywhere.

Making water easier to reach shortened one link in the chain. It did not, by itself, change who was expected to carry the rest.

Three ways to define success

As the programme closed its first phase and set directions for 2026–2030, the question had practical weight. For the water works that would follow Seo Sa Lung, three broad ways of defining success were possible.

Presented in the order used in the case report. This site does not recommend one.

Option 1

The first was to keep the existing measures: works completed, storage built, households and people served, facilities in working order. These measures were simple, comparable across provinces and already part of routine reporting. They required no new data. Every hour and every dong spent on measuring something else would be one not spent on extending pipes to places still buying water by the truckload.

Option 2

The second was to keep those measures and add a small set of gender-responsive ones: how much time households spent on water in the dry season, and who spent it; how much they paid for water they had to buy; whether girls stayed in school; how far women had a say in decisions about water. These would show more of what a reservoir changed. But they would require new data and someone to collect it, and it was not clear who that would be: commune staff, the operators of the water works, or the Women’s Union under Project 8.

Option 3

The third, and most demanding, was to build gender into the whole life of a water project: asking women and girls where and when water was needed; involving them in decisions on siting, dry-season sharing and maintenance; and judging success by what changed in their time, their schooling and their voice. This would require the water and gender components of the programme, and the agencies behind them, to plan and report together.
